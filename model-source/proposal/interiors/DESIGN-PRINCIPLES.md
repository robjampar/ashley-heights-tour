# Principles for the whole house

Owner direction, 26 September 2026: apply the research everywhere. The principal bedroom's bed/sofa/TV arrangement was particularly awkward, but the lessons are not confined to that room. Continue one room or area at a time, while checking its relationships with the rest of the house.

## Design from use, then space, then detail

Every room starts with activities and people: what happens here, who uses it together, what needs privacy, and what must be stored. Establish the main furniture and operating space before settling partitions. Judge the entrance view and the views from the positions people actually occupy. Detailed fittings, finishes and photoreal images follow a successful spatial arrangement.

The National Design Guide treats usability, comfort, privacy, accessibility and adaptability as parts of good design, together with daylight, ventilation, storage and noise. It supports evaluating these qualities together, rather than optimising floor area alone. [National Design Guide, paragraphs 124–126](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-design-guide/national-design-guide-accessible-version).

## Principles to carry into every room

1. **Give the room a clear purpose and composition.** Establish its main activity and focal element. Test alternative orientations rather than keeping the first furniture arrangement fixed while moving everything else.
2. **Draw operating space.** Include occupied chairs, open drawers, door leaves, appliance doors, exercise movements, curtains and access for cleaning. Empty-model connectivity is only an initial check.
3. **Keep circulation away from activity where possible.** Avoid the route through a kitchen's working area, through a conversation group, across a cinema screen or between a bed and its TV. Identify which routes must remain usable while another person is working or sitting.
4. **Use the best daylight and outlook deliberately.** Consider sitting, eating and working positions before allocating windows to cupboards or service spaces. Check glare, privacy, shading and useful headroom; more glazing is not automatically a better room.
5. **Arrange neighbouring functions around routines.** Consider groceries to food storage, cooking to dining, showering to dressing, bedrooms to laundry, and swimming to changing. Record which relationships are desirable and which create noise, moisture or privacy conflicts.
6. **Choose openness selectively.** Furniture, joinery and broad openings can define related activities. Enclosures remain useful where sound, light, smells, humidity or privacy require control. A glass or slatted screen is not acoustic separation.
7. **Size storage by contents and access.** Measure usable hanging, shelving and drawer capacity. Place everyday storage where the items are used. Avoid oversized rooms with poor storage frontage and inaccessible corners.
8. **Choose proportions before adding area.** A room should accommodate its intended arrangement with coherent usable space. Extra floor area in a narrow tail or unnecessary corridor may add little value.
9. **Respect the existing building.** Record known fixed elements, potentially movable elements and unknowns separately. Test service routes, structural implications, roof/headroom and openings before developing a relocation in detail. Keep the owner's preference for realistic, limited changes visible in decisions.
10. **Review at human scale.** Present the plan with operating clearances and at least the entrance view and main occupied view. A successful geometric audit does not establish beauty, comfort or construction feasibility.

These principles are project design criteria. The international dimensional references below are useful benchmarks, not a substitute for the applicable English requirements or a project-specific accessible design.

## What the principles mean in each area

| Area | Main design test | An occupied scenario to draw |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kitchen | Preparation, cooking, washing and food storage work together; social traffic stays clear | Two cooks, dishwasher open and another person heading to the garden |
| Dining | Chairs work at the table and people can still pass on the intended route | All places occupied; a chair pulled out; serving from the kitchen |
| Living / TV room | Seating supports conversation and comfortable viewing; routes go around the group | Every intended seat occupied, curtains closed, someone entering |
| Bedrooms | Clear entrance, accessible bed sides, useful outlook and storage | Making the bed, opening wardrobes, nighttime access to the bathroom |
| Principal suite | Bed, sofa and TV form an intentional arrangement; dressing and bathing support it | One person sleeping while another dresses; bed and sofa viewing positions |
| Study / loft workspace | Desk, equipment and chair fit; daylight is controllable; work has appropriate separation | Chair pulled back, equipment connected, someone passing, a call in progress |
| Bathrooms / ensuites | Dry arrival, fixture access, privacy, ventilation and cleaning | Two users, open vanity drawers, shower entry and drying |
| Gym | Equipment layout includes the manufacturer's operating and safety areas | Equipment in use, full movement range and entry/exit remaining clear |
| Cinema | Every seat has suitable screen/speaker relationships, with controlled light and sound | Reclined seats, an occupied row, entry without crossing the principal view |
| Wine bar / tasting | Serving, storage and seating work together; services remain accessible | Host serving while stools are occupied and guests circulate |
| Utility / laundry | Dirty-to-clean handling, appliance access and storage form a usable sequence | Washer door open, basket carried in, sorting/folding in use |
| Entrance / halls | Arrival, coats/shoes and onward routes are clear; views and privacy are deliberate | Two people arriving with bags, doors open, access to adjacent rooms |
| Garage / parking | Vehicle movement, doors, unloading and pedestrian access all fit | Actual vehicle sizes, doors open and groceries carried into the house |
| Pool / garden rooms | Wet/dry transitions, changing, storage and routes support use | Swimmers, occupied loungers and movement between pool, house and changing |

## Useful benchmarks and their limits

- **Kitchen:** NKBA recommends a work aisle of at least 1067 mm for one cook and 1219 mm for multiple cooks, measured between work fronts/cabinets/appliances. Appliance-opening and passing scenarios need separate checks. These are US design recommendations. [Kitchen planning guidelines, guideline 6](https://media.nkba.org/uploads/2022/05/Kitchen-Planning-Guidelines.pdf).
- **Bedrooms and dining:** CEUD's May 2025 Irish guidance includes 800 mm clear around both sides and the end of a double bed, and 1200 mm continuous space on two consecutive sides of the dining table for its UD Home level. Apply the dimension to the stated activity and design context; it is not a universal value to copy everywhere. [Internal layout checklist, printed pp. 5–6](https://universaldesign.ie/uploads/publications/UDH-checklist-Accessible-PDF-May-2025.pdf).
- **Screens and desks:** distinguish screen size/distance, screen off-axis angle and the viewer's head direction. HSE advises positioning a workstation to avoid reflections and glare. [Samsung TV viewing guidance](https://www.samsung.com/ae/support/tv-audio-video/recommended-viewing-distance-for-your-tv/) · [HSE workstation guidance](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/indg36.pdf).
- **Bathrooms:** fixture clearances and wet-room services need to be considered together; see the fuller [principal-suite research](../../revisions/interiors-principal-2026-09-26/LAYOUT-RESEARCH.md) and its NKBA and Duravit references.
- **Specialist rooms and equipment:** use the actual selected product's dimensions and operating requirements before claiming fit. Generic boxes do not verify treadmills, recliners, garage access, ventilation or pool equipment.

## Review sequence for each room

1. Confirm brief and relevant adjoining-room routines.
2. Draw the fixed shell, openings, headroom and known services.
3. Explore materially different furniture/activity arrangements.
4. Draw occupied and open-door/drawer scenarios; identify trade-offs.
5. Review entrance and occupied eye-level views.
6. Develop the selected spatial arrangement with materials and detail.
7. Recheck room access and adjoining spaces after fitting it into both proposals.

Record each check as observed, measured, assumed, or unresolved. Keep model reachability, comfort review, owner preference and construction feasibility as distinct findings.
